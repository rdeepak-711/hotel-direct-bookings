1. The spec: what is solid

Before the gaps, what is right, because these are the things to protect when day 2 runs late.

  • One AI step, checked by code. The model only reads reviews and proposes themes with quotes. Code verifies every quote against the real review, recomputes counts, and drops what it can't verify. The findings, the score and the fixes are all plain code. This is the single most defensible design decision in the project, and it is the same shape as escalation-gate, which lets you tell one story across two projects.
  • Two inferences no single site makes. The gap check (guests ask about parking in reviews; the hotel's page never mentions it) and the neighbour comparison (your mobile speed 38 against the five nearest hotels' 76). Everything else on the page is context. The plan names these as the point, and the spec builds them first, in pure logic, with tests.
  • Failure is contained per section. Only the Google place lookup can fail the run. PageSpeed, the home page, neighbours, Tripadvisor and the AI each fail alone and the report still comes out. The tests prove it.
  • Safety calls are explicit and tested. The locked-down fetch (no private networks, redirects re-checked, 8-second and 2 MB caps), review text framed as data not instructions, keys never reaching the browser, no database, no stored review text. These are the things the job post says it will judge ("architecture, reliability and security, because US hotels trust us with guest and payment data").
  • Fairness rules are written down. Neighbours counted, never named. No named hotel's bad score in public. Real reports only privately to Hotelzify.
  • Tests before code, 11 files, about 120 cases after this morning's additions, all failing with "cannot find module", which is the right reason. Codex builds to them, you review against them.
  • Honest about the thin sample. Five Google reviews per hotel (that is all the API gives) plus up to three from Tripadvisor. The report talks in counts ("2 of 8 reviews") and never in percentages, and a test enforces it.
  • The three additions from this morning close the three ways the report could be confidently wrong: grading a Facebook page as the hotel's site, calling a JavaScript shell "silent on parking", and having no number for how often the AI invents quotes.

What the CEO will probe on the call, and the answer to have ready

  • "We already make this report by hand. What's new?" The two inferences (gap check, neighbour comparison) and the fact that it runs in 30 seconds on any US hotel. Never say "new idea"; say "the automatic version of the report you hand out, plus two checks the hand version can't do at scale".
  • "Why only five reviews?" That is all Google's API gives; Tripadvisor's free plan gives three. The report says the count every time and never speaks in percentages. The batch version would use the Google Business Profile API, which gives the hotel's full review history, once a hotel connects its account. That is the day-60 item.
  • "Your tool flags booking on another domain. Our hotels book on bookmystay.io." Yes, and their own Trustpilot reviews show guests distrusting the .io domain. Raise it as an insight you found while building, with the fix (a custom domain per hotel), not as a criticism. This is the strongest single moment you can have on the call: it shows you looked at their product the way a guest does.
  • "How much does a report cost us?" About 3 to 6 US cents in Google calls and under a cent in model calls on a cheap model; see section 6. A sales team running 500 prospects a week is about $25.
  • "Where did the AI go wrong?" Have the run-log numbers: quotes offered, quotes dropped, by model. And two or three specific invented quotes from the hand check. If you have no numbers, you have no answer to the question the post says they care most about.
  • "What would you do in the first 90 days?" Month 1: this, inside the sales flow, as the free report for AAHOA leads. Day 60: batch mode plus the Google Business Profile connection. Day 90: a roadmap built from what the sales team heard from the first 50 owners who got a report. Frame every item as "what I'd check with customers first".

What to send, and in what order

  1. The live link and the repo, after the hand check, not before.
  2. Three to five founder's-version reports on real US independent hotels, sent privately. Pick hotels of different sizes and at least one that scores well, so it doesn't read as a hit piece.
  3. The short write-up: problem and evidence, what was built and left out, the security calls, where the AI went wrong (with numbers), the hand-check table.
  4. One page for a hotel owner in plain English.
  5. The 2-minute voice note, only when they ask for the challenge.
Two things that would hurt you here. (1) A dead or slow link: Google quota exhausted, PageSpeed throttled, a 504. Keep three saved reports that always load, and test the live link the morning you send it. (2) A wrong finding on a hotel the CEO knows. Hence gaps 1 and 2 above, and the hand check.

4. On your CV: does it match your niche, your office work, your passion?

The match is better than most people's side projects

Your CV already says, in the Firestorm line: "a network of attraction-ticket and destination sites, earning through GetYourGuide, Viator and Tiqets affiliate programmes". You built the pipeline behind 68 of those sites, and CrowdTracker tells travellers when to visit an attraction. Your own ventures are travel too: the itinerary tool, the Northern Lights and Bay of Kotor sites, the attraction forums. A hotel tool is the hospitality end of the same industry. A reader sees one person who has spent three years building things for travellers and the businesses that serve them, now doing it for hotels. That reads as a track, not a scattering.

The connection you can state in one sentence, and it is true: "My affiliate sites only earn when travellers find them and trust them; this tool checks the same two things for a hotel's own website." That line also works for Fibr ("agent readiness" is the same problem from the AI-reader side) and for any travel-tech company.

Where it doesn't match, said plainly

  • Your title is "AI Automation Engineer" and your target companies are Series A/B AI startups in Bangalore. Most of them don't care about hotels. For them this project has to be read as "an AI product whose every claim is checked", which is exactly how escalation-gate is read. So the CV bullet must lead with the checking, not the hotel.
  • The X strategy you set on 17 Oct says travel content is off X. This is an engineering build, so it's allowed, but keep the posts about the method (section 5) or you drift back into the thing you stopped.
  • Passion is not the same as a market. You like travel; hotel owners are not your audience and you have no way to reach them. That matters for section 6, not for the CV.

Verdict on money

Build it for the job. Its job value is high and its running cost is near zero, so there is no reason not to. Treat revenue as a two-week experiment after the Hotelzify process has run its course, and only path 1: batch mode plus one pilot conversation with a sales lead at a hotel-tech company. If that conversation doesn't produce a "yes, send me a list" within two weeks, stop and leave the tool as a portfolio piece. That keeps the rule you set on 6 Oct: products and learning yield to applications whenever they compete.

One honest note on the conflict: the "no outreach to hotel owners" rule was set for this build, so the evidence came from public data. Any revenue path means talking to someone. That's fine; the rule was about the build, not forever. But it means revenue is a different project from this one, with its own hours.
